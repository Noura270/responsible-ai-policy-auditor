ISO/IEC 42001 - AI Management System (AIMS) - Summary of Key Requirement Areas

This is an original, condensed summary of the general subject areas covered by
ISO/IEC 42001, written for internal governance-assessment use. It is NOT a
reproduction of the official standard text and should not be treated as a
substitute for the licensed ISO/IEC 42001 document.

1. Context of the Organization
An organization implementing an AI Management System (AIMS) must understand
its own context: its business objectives, the interested parties affected by
its AI systems (employees, customers, regulators, the public), and the scope
of AI systems covered by its governance program. Without a clearly defined
scope, AI systems can be deployed without anyone being accountable for their
governance.

2. Leadership and AI Policy
Top management must demonstrate visible commitment to responsible AI: a
documented AI policy, clearly assigned roles and responsibilities for AI
governance, and integration of AI risk considerations into overall business
strategy. Leadership commitment prevents AI governance from being treated as
a side task with no real ownership.

3. Planning: AI Risk and Impact Assessment
Organizations must have a systematic process to identify and evaluate risks
and opportunities related to their AI systems. This includes assessing
potential impacts on individuals and groups (bias, discrimination, safety,
privacy) and setting measurable AI objectives. This is the foundation for
any AI risk register: what could go wrong, how likely is it, and how severe
would the impact be.

4. Support: Resources, Competence, and Awareness
The organization must ensure people involved in developing, deploying, or
overseeing AI systems have the necessary competence and awareness of
responsible AI principles. Documented information (policies, procedures,
records of decisions) must be maintained and controlled so that governance
activities are traceable, not just performed informally.

5. Operation: Risk Treatment and Controls
Once risks are identified, the organization selects and implements
appropriate controls to treat them. Common control areas relevant to AI
systems include: data quality and provenance, bias and fairness testing,
system performance and robustness testing, transparency to affected users,
and human oversight mechanisms proportionate to the risk of the system.
Controls should be selected based on the specific risk profile of each AI
system, not applied uniformly regardless of context.

6. Performance Evaluation
The organization must monitor, measure, and periodically audit its AI
systems and its AIMS as a whole, and report findings to management. This
includes ongoing monitoring after deployment, not just a one-time
pre-launch assessment - AI systems can drift or behave differently as data
and usage patterns change over time.

7. Improvement
When gaps, nonconformities, or incidents are identified, the organization
must take corrective action and continually improve its AI governance
practices. A single failed assessment should trigger a documented
improvement action, not just be filed away.

Key Governance Concepts Embedded in This Standard:
- Accountability: every AI system should have a clearly assigned owner.
- Risk-based approach: the depth of controls should match the severity and
  likelihood of potential harm, not be one-size-fits-all.
- Evidence-based assurance: claims of compliance or control implementation
  should be supported by documented evidence, not just asserted.
- Continuous governance: AI governance is an ongoing operating model, not a
  one-time certification exercise - systems must be reassessed when they
  change materially (new data sources, model updates, expanded use cases).
